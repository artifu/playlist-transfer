Post-transfer guide · 8 minute read

The playlist was created. Now verify what actually arrived.

A successful API response confirms creation, not perfect catalog equivalence. Use this checklist to catch missing rows, alternate versions, order problems, and playback surprises.

Written and maintained by the PlaylistXfer project · August 2, 2026

The ten-minute rule

Spend a few minutes checking the destination immediately after creation. The match report is still fresh, the source is easy to compare, and version mistakes are faster to repair before the playlist becomes part of your normal library.

1. Confirm the destination and keep the receipt

Open Apple Music with the same account used during authorization. Look in Library → Playlists for the new private playlist. PlaylistXfer appends its product name to the source title so the destination is easier to distinguish from an older manual copy.

Before closing the browser tab, note the receipt totals: transferred, skipped, and missing. If you changed any candidate manually, remember which rows you approved. The receipt is the best explanation of why the destination count may differ from the Spotify count.

Nothing moves out of Spotify

The source remains untouched. “Transfer” means creating a new Apple Music playlist from destination-catalog songs, not deleting, converting, or relocating Spotify data.

2. Compare the right counts

Do not compare only the Spotify headline total with the final Apple Music total. A playlist can contain local files, podcasts, unavailable rows, or more tracks than the selected analysis scope. Instead, compare each stage.

CountWhat it tells you
Spotify totalAll items Spotify reports, including rows the public flow may not be able to read or transfer
Readable tracksMusic rows available to the public preview
Analyzed tracksThe rows included in the selected Full, 150-track, or 50-track scope
Ready plus approved reviewThe rows eligible for creation
Created tracksThe Apple Music catalog items accepted into the new playlist

If the created count is lower than Ready plus approved Review rows, preserve the receipt and retry only the creation step after reconnecting Apple Music. Re-running matching can produce a different report if catalog data has changed.

3. Spot-check version-sensitive songs

Start with titles containing words such as live, acoustic, remix, remaster, radio edit, demo, clean, explicit, anniversary, deluxe, or soundtrack. These descriptors are easy to miss when scanning a long list but can materially change the recording.

Also inspect songs that appear on a different album in Apple Music. A different album cover is not automatically wrong; the same ISRC can be packaged on a single, an original album, and a compilation. Listen to the opening or compare duration when the version matters.

  • Check whether explicit lyrics became a clean edit or vice versa.
  • Compare duration when a radio edit and album version share a title.
  • Verify featured artists when Apple formats them differently.
  • Listen to live, acoustic, remastered, and remix candidates.
  • Confirm classical works by performer, conductor, movement, and duration—not title alone.

4. Verify order and intentional duplicates

PlaylistXfer creates tracks in the analyzed source order after excluded rows are removed. That means the order should remain stable, but positions will close up around missing or skipped items. Compare a few landmarks: the first three songs, a group from the middle, and the final three.

Do not delete every duplicate automatically. Some source playlists intentionally repeat a theme, include both studio and live versions, or use the same recording more than once. PlaylistXfer treats source rows as separate decisions, so an intended duplicate can remain in the destination.

If the playlist is sequenced for a workout, event, DJ set, or story, check transitions around missing rows. A technically correct omission can still create an awkward jump that deserves a manual replacement.

5. Repair missing songs manually—with the same caution

Open the Missing section of the report and search Apple Music using title plus primary artist. If the result is weak, add album name, featured artist, or version words. When several candidates look plausible, compare duration and listen before adding one.

A missing status can mean the recording is absent from the storefront, but it can also mean the available metadata was not strong enough for automatic selection. Manual search gives you room to accept an alternate edition that an automated threshold should reject.

Some rows cannot be repaired inside Apple Music: Spotify local files, podcasts, removed releases, and recordings unavailable in the destination country may have no catalog equivalent. Keep a short note or a separate “missing from transfer” list if those items matter.

6. Test playback on the device that matters

Play several songs from the destination playlist on the device you normally use. A catalog item can appear in search but still be unavailable, greyed out, explicit-content restricted, or mismatched to the account’s current storefront.

If Apple Music displays the playlist but refuses to play many rows, confirm the account, subscription, region, and content restrictions before repeating the transfer. Recreating the same playlist will not solve an account-level playback problem.

Once the spot checks pass, rename the playlist or add a description inside Apple Music if you want to remove the transfer suffix. Keep the Spotify source until you are satisfied; there is no benefit to deleting the original during verification.

A compact final checklist

  1. Open the correct Apple Music account and find the new playlist.
  2. Compare receipt totals with analyzed and eligible rows.
  3. Spot-check version-sensitive and compilation matches.
  4. Verify the beginning, middle, and end of the track order.
  5. Keep intentional duplicates and repair missing songs cautiously.
  6. Test actual playback before treating the transfer as finished.
